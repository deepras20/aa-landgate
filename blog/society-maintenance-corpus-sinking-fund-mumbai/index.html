Society Maintenance, Corpus Fund & Sinking Fund
What Every Mumbai Buyer Must Know

The purchase price printed on your sale agreement is only the first number a flat buyer in Mumbai needs to know. Every housing society in Maharashtra comes with a set of mandatory financial obligations that begin on the day you receive possession — and understanding them before you sign could save you a significant amount of money and future frustration.

What Are Society Maintenance Charges?

Society maintenance charges are the monthly fees every flat owner pays to their Cooperative Housing Society (CHS) to cover the ongoing upkeep of the building and common areas. These charges fund the salaries of security guards, housekeeping staff, and a building manager; electricity for common areas, lifts, and water pumps; garden and amenity upkeep; and routine repairs to the building’s common infrastructure.

In Mumbai, Thane, and Mira Road East, maintenance charges for a typical 2BHK flat range from ₹2,500 to ₹6,000 per month, depending on the building’s age, amenities, and location. Premium projects with a gym, swimming pool, and 24-hour concierge can charge ₹8,000–₹15,000 per month or more. Under the Maharashtra Cooperative Societies Act, the society’s general body sets and approves the maintenance amount annually. As a member, you have the right to attend the Annual General Meeting (AGM) and vote on the charges proposed.

What Is the Corpus Fund?

The corpus fund is a one-time, non-refundable deposit paid by the buyer at the time of flat purchase — not a recurring monthly charge. It forms the society’s reserve capital: a financial cushion for emergency repairs, legal costs, or large one-off expenditures that fall outside the monthly maintenance budget. Think of it as the society’s savings account.

The corpus fund amount is set by the builder or developer at the time of project launch and is typically included in the final cost sheet presented to buyers. In Mumbai’s market, corpus fund contributions generally range from ₹50,000 to ₹2,00,000 per flat, depending on project size, location, and amenities offered. Under MahaRERA regulations, the corpus fund collected by a builder must be handed over to the housing society once it is formally registered — it cannot be retained by the developer or used for construction costs. Always confirm the corpus fund amount in writing in your allotment letter.

What Is the Sinking Fund?

The sinking fund is a mandatory reserve built up through regular monthly contributions from all flat owners, specifically earmarked for major structural repairs and capital expenditure — such as waterproofing, lift replacement, external painting, terrace repair, or rewiring of the building’s electrical system. Unlike maintenance charges, which cover day-to-day running costs, the sinking fund accumulates over years to pay for the larger, infrequent but unavoidable costs of maintaining an ageing building.

The Maharashtra Cooperative Societies Act mandates that every registered housing society must maintain a sinking fund. The minimum required contribution is 0.25% of the construction cost of each flat per year, collected monthly. For an older building in Mira Road East or Bhayandar where the construction cost was ₹30 lakh, that works out to roughly ₹625 per month added to your maintenance bill specifically for the sinking fund. Newer premium projects set higher contributions in line with today’s replacement costs for fixtures and fittings.

How Much Should You Budget Beyond the Purchase Price?

Savvy buyers in Mumbai account for all society-related costs when calculating their total cost of ownership. Here is a realistic planning framework for a 2BHK in a mid-segment society in Mira Road East or Thane:

  • Corpus fund (one-time at possession): ₹75,000 – ₹1,50,000
  • Monthly maintenance (ongoing): ₹3,000 – ₹5,000 per month
  • Sinking fund component (included in maintenance): ₹500 – ₹1,000 per month
  • Property tax (annual, paid to municipal body): ₹5,000 – ₹15,000 per year depending on carpet area and location

Over a ten-year horizon, the cumulative maintenance charges alone on a typical 2BHK in Mira Road East or Kharghar, Navi Mumbai can amount to ₹4–7 lakh — a material figure that buyers who focus only on the purchase price often overlook entirely when planning their finances.

Your Rights as a Society Member

Once you are a registered member of a Cooperative Housing Society in Maharashtra, you have clear legal rights. You are entitled to inspect the society’s accounts and audit reports; vote at the AGM on maintenance charges, sinking fund contributions, and large expenditure proposals; stand for election to the managing committee; and raise a formal complaint with the Deputy Registrar of Cooperative Societies if you believe charges are being levied improperly. If your society is not yet registered — common in newer buildings during the transition from builder to residents — a deemed conveyance application can be filed to transfer property rights to the society, and the builder is legally obligated to facilitate this process within a set timeline under MahaRERA rules.

When evaluating any flat in Mumbai, Panvel, Andheri, or Mira-Bhayandar, always ask the seller or builder for the current maintenance charges, the corpus fund amount already collected, the sinking fund balance, and the most recent AGM minutes. These documents tell you the financial health of the society you are about to join — and any unexplained arrears or large pending repairs are a red flag worth investigating before you commit.

“We always tell our clients: the sale agreement gives you the flat, but the society’s financial health gives you the quality of life inside it. A building with a healthy sinking fund and timely maintenance is worth more in ten years than a building that deferred every repair.
